Title: All Things Considered
Author: G. K. Chesterton
Source: Project Gutenberg (public domain)

ON RUNNING AFTER ONE'S HAT. I feel an almost savage contempt for the special kind of person who is always saying that these are prosaic and humdrum times. Nothing can be more wildly and preposterously sensational than an ordinary human life. The adventure of the hat is not merely an adventure. It is the symbol of an adventure.

ON LYING IN BED. Lying in bed would be an altogether perfect and supreme experience if only one had a colored pencil long enough to draw on the ceiling. The recumbent position is exactly the right attitude for philosophy. Gazing at the ceiling, one is forced to meditate on great permanent things.

ON THE FALLACY OF SUCCESS. There has appeared in our time a particular class of books which I sincerely and solemnly think is the most dangerous of all — the books about Success. They are dangerous because they confuse two quite distinct things — the way to succeed and the way to be happy.

ON THE COMIC SPIRIT. What is merely amusing passes with the moment; what is funny endures. The reason is that genuine humor is always connected with truth — with some perception of reality that strikes us suddenly and surprises us into laughter. The man who laughs at a thing has generally understood it.
